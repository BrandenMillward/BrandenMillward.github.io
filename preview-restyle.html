How agent networks fit together

Most organisations don't have an AI problem. They have a trust problem.

The models are good enough. They have been for a while. What stops an AI system reaching production in a bank, an insurer, or a national infrastructure programme is rarely accuracy — it's that nobody can explain what the thing did, or say who is accountable when it is wrong.

That gap is where I've spent my career. I started as an analyst at Capital One building models nobody acted on, learned the hard way that a model nobody trusts is an expensive calculation, and spent the years since moving up the stack — through platform governance at Experian, transformation at the Post Office, and AI strategy consulting at Cloud Formations — to the point where the architecture, the explainability, and the organisational change are all one job.

Today I'm an AI Orchestration Architect at Intent HQ. I design agent networks: the roles each agent plays, how they hand work to each other, the prompts and protocols that hold it together, and the guardrails that stop it drifting. The viewport above is a live sketch of that idea — nodes passing messages, an orchestrator holding the middle.

My MSc dissertation asked whether explainability techniques could close the deep learning trust gap in regulated industries, using 2.26 million real credit decisions. The answer was yes, and the finding has shaped everything since: the barrier to adoption is auditability, not capability.

I write and speak about this a lot — most recently at SQLBits 2026 — because the hardest part of this work has never been the technology.
